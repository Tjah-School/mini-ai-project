## 1. What Is Symbolic AI?

Symbolic AI is an approach to artificial intelligence in which a computer reasons by manipulating **symbols** (such as words, labels, numbers, and logical statements) according to **explicit rules** written by humans.

This approach is also called **Good Old-Fashioned AI (GOFAI)**. The term was popularized by the philosopher John Haugeland in 1985 to describe the first major paradigm of AI research.

In plain language: a human expert writes down what they know as rules, and the computer follows those rules step by step to reach conclusions.

### A simple example

Consider a small medical helper:

```text
Rule 1: IF the patient has a fever AND a skin rash, THEN suspect measles.
Rule 2: IF measles is suspected AND there is no vaccination record, THEN recommend a blood test.
```

No one trained this program on thousands of patient records. A human wrote the knowledge directly, and the program combines the rules to derive a conclusion. Because the rules are visible, the system can explain which rules it used.

Notice that the output is only as good as the rules. The program does not diagnose anyone in the real world; it applies the knowledge it was given.

## 2. The Physical Symbol System Hypothesis

In 1976, Allen Newell and Herbert Simon stated the idea that became the philosophical foundation of GOFAI, known as the **Physical Symbol System Hypothesis (PSSH)**:

> A physical symbol system has the necessary and sufficient means for general intelligent action.

### Breaking the idea into parts

- **Physical:** the symbols exist in the real world, for example as electrical states in a computer or, according to the hypothesis, as patterns in a brain.
- **Symbol:** a pattern that stands for something else.
- **System:** the symbols are organized into structures (like sentences or equations) and changed by processes (like copying, combining, comparing, and deleting).

### Two claims inside one sentence

- **Necessary:** anything that is intelligent must work as a symbol system. Taken seriously, this includes human thinking.
- **Sufficient:** a symbol system with the right organization and programming can be intelligent. This claim justified building AI on ordinary computers.

### In plain language

The hypothesis gave researchers a clear research program: if thinking is symbol manipulation, then machines might be built to think by programming symbol manipulation.

### Example

| Real-world concept | Symbolic representation |
| --- | --- |
| A person named Alice | `ALICE` |
| Alice belongs to the student category | `Student(ALICE)` |
| Every student is a learner | `Student(x) → Learner(x)` |
| Alice is a learner | `Learner(ALICE)` |

## 3. Why Did Early Researchers Focus on Logic?

Several reasons made logic and explicit rules an attractive foundation in the 1950s:

1. **Deep intellectual roots.** For centuries, thinkers treated reasoning as rule-following. Examples include Aristotle's syllogisms, Leibniz's idea of a calculus of reasoning, and George Boole's 1854 work *The Laws of Thought*, which expressed logic in algebraic form. Later, Frege, Russell, and Whitehead developed formal logic as a foundation for mathematics.
2. **Computers manipulate symbols.** Alan Turing's 1936 work on computation showed how a machine following simple rules on symbols can carry out any computation that can be precisely described. Computers seemed a natural fit for symbolic reasoning.
3. **Logic resembles deliberate thinking.** When people solve a math problem or plan a trip, they often seem to follow steps and rules. Early researchers took this as a model of intelligence.
4. **Limited data and hardware.** There was no internet-scale data, and computers had very small memories and slow processors. Learning from millions of examples was not practical, so writing knowledge by hand was the realistic path.
5. **Precision and verifiability.** Logical conclusions can be traced and checked step by step.

**Example:** the classic syllogism

- All humans are mortal.
- Socrates is a human.
- Therefore, Socrates is mortal.

Early researchers hoped that a machine doing this kind of reasoning over very large collections of facts and rules could reach general intelligence.

## 4. The 1956 Dartmouth Conference

Symbolic AI is often said to have begun at a summer workshop held at Dartmouth College in 1956. It was organized by John McCarthy, Marvin Minsky, Nathaniel Rochester, and Claude Shannon.

The workshop proposal stated the belief that every aspect of learning or any other feature of intelligence could, in principle, be described so precisely that a machine could be made to simulate it. McCarthy is credited with introducing the term **"artificial intelligence"** for this effort. Newell and Simon presented their Logic Theorist program there.

The event is widely regarded as the founding moment of AI as a research field.

## 5. Early Symbolic AI Programs

| Year | Milestone | What it showed |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1950 | Turing's paper *Computing Machinery and Intelligence* | Raised the question "Can machines think?" and proposed the imitation game |
| 1955-56 | Logic Theorist (Newell, Simon, Shaw) | Often called the first AI program; it proved many theorems from *Principia Mathematica* |
| 1957 | General Problem Solver (GPS) | Attempted a general method for solving well-defined problems |
| 1958 | LISP (John McCarthy) | A programming language designed for symbol manipulation |
| 1965 | DENDRAL (collaborative team of researchers) | Early system for identifying molecular structures using expert knowledge |
| 1966 | ELIZA (Joseph Weizenbaum) | Imitated a conversation partner using pattern-matching rules |
| Late 1960s | SHRDLU (Terry Winograd) | Followed English commands in a small "blocks world" |

### Logic Theorist

The Logic Theorist proved 38 of the first 52 theorems in *Principia Mathematica*, according to commonly cited accounts. It showed that a program could carry out reasoning that people associated with human mathematicians.

### General Problem Solver and means-ends analysis

GPS used a technique called **means-ends analysis**: compare the current state with the goal state, identify the difference, and choose an action that reduces it.

For example, if a robot is in the kitchen and needs to be in the garage, the difference is its location, so it selects an action such as "walk to the garage."

### LISP

LISP became a major language for AI programming for decades because it treats symbolic expressions as data that programs can easily manipulate.

### ELIZA and SHRDLU

ELIZA created a convincing conversation using simple pattern-matching rules, even though it did not understand the conversation. SHRDLU could follow commands such as "pick up the red block," but only inside a tiny, carefully defined artificial world. Both illustrate the promise and the limits of early symbolic systems.

## 6. Early Optimism

Success in checkers, chess-like games, and mathematical proofs led many early researchers to believe human-level intelligence might be close. In 1965, Herbert Simon predicted that within twenty years machines would be capable of doing any work a person can do. Marvin Minsky also made optimistic predictions during this period.

These predictions proved far too optimistic. Success in small, well-defined problems did not automatically scale to real-world complexity.

## 7. The First AI Winter

When early systems failed to scale from small "toy" problems to messy real-world tasks, confidence and funding declined. This period of reduced interest and support, roughly the mid-1970s, is known as the **first AI Winter**.

Commonly cited causes include:

- **Combinatorial explosion:** the number of possibilities grows extremely fast as problems get larger, overwhelming the computers of the time.
- **Brittleness:** a rule-based program can fail badly when it meets a situation its rules did not anticipate.
- **The common-sense problem:** people know a huge number of obvious facts (for example, "water is wet" or "you cannot walk through a wall") that are very hard to write down as rules.

The 1973 Lighthill Report in the United Kingdom criticized the lack of practical results in AI research, and funding agencies such as DARPA in the United States became more cautious. A second period of reduced enthusiasm followed in the late 1980s, when expert systems proved costly to maintain and the market for specialized AI hardware declined.

## 8. From General Reasoning to Expert Systems

In the 1970s, researchers increasingly concluded that general-purpose reasoning was not enough. Real expertise depends on large amounts of **specific knowledge**. This shifted attention toward **expert systems**: programs that capture the knowledge of human specialists as rules.

### Main parts of an expert system

- **Knowledge base:** the facts and rules supplied by experts.
- **Inference engine:** the mechanism that applies rules to facts to derive conclusions.
- **Explanation facility:** a component that can show which rules led to a conclusion.

```text
Knowledge base (facts + rules) → Inference engine → Conclusion + explanation
```

### Well-known examples

- **MYCIN** (early 1970s, Stanford): recommended diagnoses and treatments for certain blood infections using several hundred rules.
- **XCON / R1** (around 1980, Digital Equipment Corporation): helped configure computer system orders.
- **Prolog** (1972): a programming language in which the programmer states facts and rules, and the system works out answers using logical inference.

During the 1980s, interest and investment in expert systems grew, and companies and governments funded AI projects, including Japan's Fifth Generation Computer Systems project launched in 1982.

## 9. Philosophical Critiques

In 1980, philosopher John Searle proposed the **Chinese Room** thought experiment. Imagine a person who does not understand Chinese, locked in a room with a rulebook for responding to Chinese messages. By following the rules, the person produces replies that look fluent, yet the person understands nothing.

Searle argued that a program manipulating symbols has **syntax** (rules for arranging symbols) but not necessarily **semantics** (meaning), so symbol manipulation alone may not amount to real understanding. This argument is debated, and many researchers have offered replies.

A related issue is the **symbol grounding problem**, discussed by Stevan Harnad in 1990: how do symbols get their meaning if they are defined only in terms of other symbols?

## 9. Legacy of Symbolic AI

Symbolic AI did not deliver general human-level intelligence, but its ideas remain useful:

- **Search and planning algorithms** appear in navigation, games, and logistics.
- **Rule engines** are used in areas such as finance, insurance, and compliance software.
- **Knowledge representation** influenced modern knowledge graphs.
- **Programming languages and tools** such as LISP influenced later language design.
- **Neuro-symbolic and hybrid AI** research combines learning from data with explicit rules and reasoning.

## 10. Summary

- Symbolic AI, or GOFAI, began in the 1950s as the first major AI paradigm.
- It rests on the Physical Symbol System Hypothesis: intelligence can be achieved by manipulating symbols using explicit rules and logic.
- Early researchers favored logic because of its intellectual history, its fit with computers, its precision, and the lack of data and computing power for learning-based methods.
- The 1956 Dartmouth Conference marked the founding of the field.
- Brittleness, combinatorial explosion, and the common-sense problem contributed to the first AI Winter.
- Early programs produced striking results, and expert systems made symbolic AI practical in the 1970s and 1980s.
